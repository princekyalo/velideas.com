1.1 What is cryptocurrency?

Cryptocurrency, commonly known as crypto, is a form of digital currency that uses cryptography to secure transactions and ownership. Most cryptocurrencies operate on decentralised networks called blockchains.

Unlike traditional currencies such as the Kenyan shilling or US dollar, many cryptocurrencies are not issued or controlled by a central bank. Instead, transactions are verified and recorded by a distributed network of computers.

For example, Bitcoin allows people to transfer value to one another without necessarily using a traditional bank as an intermediary.





<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
1.2 Understanding blockchain technology</strong>

A blockchain is a digital ledger that records transactions in blocks. These blocks are connected chronologically, forming a chain of transaction records.

Think of blockchain as a digital record book that is shared across multiple computers. Once transactions are confirmed and added to the blockchain, altering historical records becomes extremely difficult.
</p>





<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
Key characteristics of blockchain:</strong>

- Decentralisation: Information is maintained by a distributed network rather than one central authority.
- Transparency: Transactions on many public blockchains can be viewed by anyone.
- Security: Cryptographic techniques help protect transaction records.
- Immutability: Confirmed records are generally difficult to alter.
- Peer-to-peer transactions: Users can transact directly without necessarily relying on traditional intermediaries.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
1.3 What makes cryptocurrency valuable?
</strong>

Cryptocurrency derives value from different factors, including:

1. Supply and demand: When demand increases relative to available supply, prices may rise.
2. Utility: Some cryptocurrencies provide access to services, applications or blockchain networks.
3. Scarcity: Certain cryptocurrencies have limited supplies. Bitcoin, for example, has a maximum supply of 21 million coins.
4. Adoption: Increased acceptance and use can increase demand.
5. Market sentiment: Investor confidence, speculation and market expectations influence prices.

Remember that having a limited supply or an interesting technology does not automatically make a cryptocurrency a good investment.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
1.4 Bitcoin versus traditional currencies

Bitcoin is different from traditional currencies in several ways:

Bitcoin| Traditional currency
Digital asset| May exist as physical cash and digital balances
Operates on a blockchain| Issued within a central banking and monetary system
Maximum supply of 21 million| Supply is managed through monetary policy
Transactions can be peer-to-peer| Often relies on financial intermediaries
Operates continuously| Banking services may have operating hours
</p>

MODULE 2: TYPES OF CRYPTOCURRENCIES

<p>
Not all cryptocurrencies serve the same purpose. Understanding their different categories helps investors identify what they are buying.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.1 Payment cryptocurrencies
</strong>

These are cryptocurrencies designed primarily to facilitate the transfer of value between users.

Examples:

- Bitcoin (BTC)
- Litecoin (LTC)
- Bitcoin Cash (BCH)

Bitcoin is the best-known example. It was designed as a peer-to-peer electronic payment system and is also widely treated as a store-of-value asset.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.2 Smart contract and blockchain platform coins
</strong>

These cryptocurrencies power blockchain networks that support applications, smart contracts and other digital services.

Examples:

- Ethereum (ETH)
- Solana (SOL)
- BNB (BNB)
- Cardano (ADA)

For example, Ethereum allows developers to build decentralised applications and smart contracts. ETH is used within its ecosystem to pay transaction fees and participate in network activities.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.3 stablecoins
</strong>

Stablecoins are cryptocurrencies designed to maintain a relatively stable value by referencing another asset, commonly the US dollar.

Examples:

- USDT (Tether)
- USDC (USD Coin)
- DAI

One USDT is generally designed to maintain a value close to one US dollar.

Stablecoins are particularly useful in trading because they allow traders to move between volatile cryptocurrencies and dollar-referenced digital assets without necessarily converting back to traditional currency.

However, stablecoins still carry risks, including issuer, reserve, liquidity and regulatory risks.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.4 Meme coins
</strong>

Meme coins are cryptocurrencies that often originate from internet culture, jokes or online communities.

Examples:

- Dogecoin (DOGE)
- Shiba Inu (SHIB)
- Pepe (PEPE)

Their prices can be heavily influenced by social media attention, celebrity endorsements, speculation and community sentiment.

Meme coins are particularly risky because their market prices may not be supported by strong underlying utility or economic fundamentals.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.5 Utility and governance tokens
</strong>

These tokens provide specific functions within blockchain projects.

- Utility tokens: Provide access to services or functions within a particular ecosystem.
- Governance tokens: Give eligible holders voting rights on certain project decisions.

Examples include UNI (Uniswap) and LINK (Chainlink).

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
2.6 Non-fungible tokens (NFTs)
</strong>

NFTs are unique digital tokens representing ownership or claims associated with particular digital or physical items.

They can represent:

- Digital artwork.
- Collectibles.
- Gaming assets.
- Memberships.
- Certain real-world assets.

Unlike ordinary cryptocurrencies, individual NFTs are not necessarily interchangeable on a one-to-one basis.
</p>














MODULE 3: CRYPTOCURRENCY WALLETS




<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
3.1 What is a crypto wallet?
</strong>

A cryptocurrency wallet is a tool that allows users to manage their cryptocurrency and interact with blockchain networks.

A wallet does not necessarily store coins physically. Instead, it manages the cryptographic keys that give users control over their blockchain assets.

There are two important concepts:

Public address: Similar to a bank account number. You can share it with someone who wants to send you cryptocurrency.

Private key: Similar to a highly sensitive password that gives control over your cryptocurrency. Never share it.

A recovery phrase, also called a seed phrase, is a set of words that can be used to restore access to a wallet. Anyone who obtains it may be able to control the funds.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  

3.2 Types of cryptocurrency wallets
</strong>

A. Hot wallets

Hot wallets are connected to the internet.

Examples:

- Binance Web3 Wallet.
- Trust Wallet.
- MetaMask.

Advantages:

- Convenient for everyday transactions.
- Easy to access.
- Useful for interacting with decentralised applications.

Disadvantages:

- More exposed to online threats.
- Vulnerable to phishing and malicious applications.
- Security depends heavily on the user's practices.

B. Cold wallets

Cold wallets keep private keys offline or otherwise isolated from routine internet-connected use.

Examples:

- Ledger hardware wallets.
- Trezor hardware wallets.

Advantages:

- Reduced exposure to online attacks.
- Suitable for long-term storage.
- Greater control over private keys.

Disadvantages:

- May require purchasing hardware.
- Can be less convenient for frequent transactions.
- Users must protect recovery phrases and physical devices.


</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  

3.3 Custodial versus non-custodial wallets
</strong>

These are two important wallet management arrangements.

Custodial wallet: A third party controls the private keys on your behalf. For example, when you leave cryptocurrency in your Binance exchange account, Binance manages the custody of those assets.

Non-custodial wallet: You control your own private keys and are responsible for protecting them.

The distinction is important: keeping cryptocurrency on an exchange is not the same as holding it in a personal wallet.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  

3.4 Wallet security
</strong>

Always observe the following precautions:

1. Never share your private keys or recovery phrase.
2. Activate two-factor authentication (2FA).
3. Use strong, unique passwords.
4. Verify wallet addresses before sending funds.
5. Be careful with suspicious links and applications.
6. Consider a hardware wallet for significant long-term holdings.
7. Understand the blockchain network before transferring assets.

A transaction sent to the wrong network or address may be difficult or impossible to recover.
</p>








HOW TO MAKE MONEY FROM CRYPTO
<p>
There are several ways to generate returns from cryptocurrency. Each has different risks, capital requirements and time commitments.
</p>




<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  

4.1 Buy and hold (long-term investing)
</strong>

This involves purchasing a cryptocurrency and holding it for an extended period, with the expectation that its value will appreciate.

For example:

- Buy Bitcoin at $60,000.
- Hold it for several months or years.
- Sell it at $80,000.

The potential profit is $20,000 per Bitcoin, before fees and other costs.

The strategy is sometimes referred to as HODLing.

It is important to remember that the price can also decline significantly during the holding period.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.2 Spot trading
</strong>
Spot trading involves buying and selling actual cryptocurrency assets at prevailing market prices.

Example:

- Buy ETH at $2,000.
- Sell ETH at $2,200.
- Gross profit: $200 per ETH.

Spot trading is a suitable starting point for beginners because it does not require borrowing money or using leverage.
</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.3 Day trading
</strong>
Day trading involves opening and closing trading positions within the same day to benefit from short-term price movements.

A day trader may make several trades in one day, looking for relatively small price movements.

It requires:

- Technical analysis.
- Market monitoring.
- Strict risk management.
- Discipline and emotional control.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.4 Swing trading
</strong>

Swing trading involves holding positions for several days or weeks to benefit from larger price movements.

For example, a trader may identify an upward trend in Bitcoin, buy during a temporary price correction and sell after the price recovers.

Swing trading generally requires less constant monitoring than day trading.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.5 Staking
</strong>
Staking involves committing eligible cryptocurrency to support the operations of certain proof-of-stake blockchain networks.

In return, participants may receive staking rewards.

For example, eligible ETH holders can participate in Ethereum staking.

However, staking returns are not guaranteed. Risks may include price volatility, lock-up periods, validator penalties and platform-related risks.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.6 Mining
</strong>

Mining involves using computing power to validate transactions and secure proof-of-work blockchain networks.

Bitcoin is the most prominent example.

Miners compete to solve computational problems and may receive block rewards and transaction fees.

Mining requires:

- Specialised equipment.
- Electricity.
- Technical knowledge.
- Appropriate cooling and maintenance.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.7 Arbitrage trading
</strong>

Arbitrage involves attempting to profit from price differences for the same cryptocurrency across different markets or exchanges.

For example:

- Exchange A sells BTC at $60,000.
- Exchange B sells BTC at $60,300.
- A trader attempts to buy at A and sell at B.

The potential profit is $300 before fees, transfer costs, slippage and execution risks.

Price differences can disappear quickly.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
4.8 Other opportunities
</strong>

Other crypto-related opportunities include:

- Participating in eligible token launches.
- Providing liquidity in decentralised finance (DeFi).
- Participating in certain airdrops.
- Developing blockchain applications.
- Providing blockchain-related professional services.

These opportunities require additional research and may expose participants to substantial risks.

Important: Making money in crypto is not guaranteed. Trading, investing and earning programmes can all result in losses. Avoid anyone promising fixed or guaranteed returns.

</p>

UNDERSTANDING CRYPTOCURRENCY TRADING



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
6.1 What is trading?
</strong>

Trading involves buying and selling financial assets with the intention of profiting from changes in their market prices.

A trader attempts to identify opportunities where an asset may increase or decrease in value.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
6.2 Bullish and bearish markets
</strong>

Bull market: A market characterised by a sustained upward trend in prices.

Bear market: A market characterised by a sustained downward trend in prices.

Other important terms:

- Bullish: Expecting prices to increase.
- Bearish: Expecting prices to decrease.
- Long position: A position that benefits when the asset price rises.
- Short position: A position that benefits when the asset price falls.
- Volatility: The degree and speed of price fluctuations.
- Liquidity: How easily an asset can be bought or sold without significantly affecting its price.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
6.3 Types of trading orders
</strong>

When trading on Binance Spot, you will encounter different order types.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
A. Market order
</strong>

A market order buys or sells an asset immediately at the best available market prices.

Example:

Bitcoin is trading around $60,000. You place a market buy order and receive Bitcoin at the available execution prices.

The final execution price may differ slightly from the displayed price due to slippage.
</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
B. Limit order
</strong>
A limit order allows you to specify the price at which you want to buy or sell.

Example:

Bitcoin is trading at $60,000, but you only want to buy at $58,000.

You place a limit buy order at $58,000. The order executes only if matching market conditions occur.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
C. Stop-loss order
</strong>

A stop-loss is a risk-management instruction designed to close a position when the market reaches a specified price.

Example:

- Buy Bitcoin at $60,000.
- Set a stop-loss at $57,000.
- Your intended exit is triggered if the stop condition is reached.

Depending on the order type and market conditions, the final execution price may be worse than the stop price.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
D. Take-profit order
</strong>

A take-profit order is designed to close a position after the market reaches a predetermined profit target.

Example:

- Buy Bitcoin at $60,000.
- Set a take-profit target at $66,000.

The purpose is to help lock in gains according to a predefined trading plan.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
6.4 Understanding trading timeframes
</strong>

Charts can display price movements over different periods.

Common timeframes include:

- 1-minute chart: Very short-term movements.
- 5-minute chart: Short-term trading.
- 15-minute chart: Intraday analysis.
- 1-hour chart: Intraday and short-term trends.
- 4-hour chart: Swing trading analysis.
- Daily chart: Broader market trends.
- Weekly chart: Long-term market direction.

Beginners should avoid making decisions based entirely on very short timeframes because they contain considerable market noise.

</p>










TECHNICAL ANALYSIS



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
7.1 What is technical analysis?
</strong>

Technical analysis (TA) involves studying historical price movements, trading volume and chart patterns to identify potential future market movements.

The main assumption is that market prices reflect the combined actions and expectations of market participants.

Technical analysis does not predict the future with certainty. It helps traders identify probabilities and manage their decisions.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
7.2 Understanding candlestick charts
</strong>

Candlestick charts are among the most widely used tools in cryptocurrency trading.

Each candle represents price movements over a selected period.

A candlestick displays four important values:

- Open: The price at the beginning of the period.
- High: The highest price reached.
- Low: The lowest price reached.
- Close: The price at the end of the period.

A typical green candle indicates that the closing price was higher than the opening price.

A typical red candle indicates that the closing price was lower than the opening price.

The candle consists of:

1. The body, representing the opening and closing prices.
2. The upper wick, showing the highest price.
3. The lower wick, showing the lowest price.

</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
7.3 Support and resistance
</strong>

These are two of the most important concepts in technical analysis.

Support

A support level is a price area where buying pressure has historically been strong enough to prevent or slow further price declines.

Resistance

A resistance level is a price area where selling pressure has historically been strong enough to prevent or slow further price increases.

Example:

Bitcoin has repeatedly struggled to fall below $58,000 and has repeatedly struggled to rise above $65,000.

In this example:

- $58,000 represents a potential support area.
- $65,000 represents a potential resistance area.

These levels are zones rather than guaranteed turning points.
</p>



<p>
<strong class="subtopic-title">  
7.4 Trend analysis
</strong>

There are three main market trends:

Uptrend: Characterised by higher highs and higher lows.

Downtrend: Characterised by lower highs and lower lows.

Sideways trend: Prices move within a relatively defined range without a clear upward or downward direction.

Identifying the broader trend helps traders avoid trading against prevailing market momentum without a clear reason.

</p>
